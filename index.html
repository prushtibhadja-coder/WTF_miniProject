Guide

What this app is, how to use it, and exactly where its limits are.

What this is

The Eighth Schedule of the Indian Constitution names 22 languages. This app gives each one a page: eighty-nine everyday words and phrases arranged by difficulty, the script it is written in, who speaks it and where, and a short reference section on its history and literature.

1,958 phrases in all. It runs from a single HTML file with no account, no server and no tracking, and works with the network switched off.

How to learn with it

  1. Explore — pick a language, or search by name, script or state.
  2. Point at a phrase to hear it, then use the tick to mark it learned.
  3. Practice — mixed questions across all 22, in both directions.
  4. Progress — see what has stuck. It is saved in this browser only.

Hearing the words

Audio comes from the voices installed on your device, so coverage varies. The Listen tab lists all 22 and lets you assign any installed voice to any language; the mic records your own take and plays it back against the device voice.

Moving a voice between devices: record or generate on the machine that has the voice, press Export my voice in the recorder, and import that file on the other one. The audio travels in the file, not in this HTML.

Twelve of the 22 usually have no voice on a desktop: Assamese, Bodo, Dogri, Kashmiri, Konkani, Maithili, Manipuri, Odia, Punjabi, Sanskrit, Santali and Sindhi. Each of those pages now says so and offers to record your own, or to read the script with a related voice — approximate, but enough to hear the letters.

Opened the file straight from disk? The browser will not give it microphone access. Either serve the folder — python3 -m http.server 8000, then http://localhost:8000/… — or record on a phone and use Import audio.

You can also record phrases yourself: Record my voice on the Listen tab or inside any phrasebook keeps your own takes in this browser, and they take priority over every other voice.

Where a language has no device voice, use Add a real voice on the Listen tab: paste a Sarvam key and the app records the phrases and keeps the audio in this browser. voice-studio.html does the same job and bakes the audio into a portable file.

What to trust

Speaker figures are 2011 Census counts and are now dated. The phrases were produced with a machine translation model: good for practice, not authoritative, and worth checking with a native speaker before you teach from them. Classical-language status is given with its year.

Keyboard

  • Esc closes an open phrasebook.
  • Enter or Space opens the focused language card.
  • Card names, phrases and answer options can all be heard on hover.

The three files

  • bhasha-22-languages.html — the app itself.
  • voice-studio.html — browser tool that records a voice pack with your own Sarvam key.
  • make_voice_pack.py — the same job as a script, for a terminal.

Practice

Questions are drawn from all 22 phrasebooks, mixing directions: from English into the language, and from the language back into English.
